Overview

The policy is trained for 20k steps with a batch size of 64 on one RTX 5090 (29 min). Everything is trained from scratch except the DINOv2 image encoder, which starts from its pretrained weights and is fine-tuned. The policy is conditioned on the wrist camera, the action flow and the robot state, and generates the next 32 action steps (1.6 s at 20 Hz).

  • Robot state (9 values): gripper position x, y, z in the sim world; gripper orientation as a quaternion (4, near constant, gripper pointing down); finger positions (2), how far each of the two fingers has slid out from the centre, 0 (closed) to 4 cm (fully open).
  • Action (4 values per step): gripper move dx, dy, dz in [−1, 1], where 1 asks the arm controller for 5 cm in one 20 Hz step; gripper command −1 (open) or +1 (close). The orientation is never changed.

Results

  • All policies are trained for 20k steps (batch 64).
  • At evaluation the policy predicts 32 actions, the first 10 are run, then it predicts again.
  • Each episode is run 5 times for each of 5 seeds (25 runs). Tables show the success rate (%), mean ± std over the 5 seeds.
  • Flow-conditioned evals use the ground truth action flow from each episode's data, aligned to the sim's clock.

  • Baselines are standard visuomotor policies: the same model with no action flow conditioning.
  • The baseline with only the wrist camera scores poorly: 59 % train / 37 % test, against 84 % / 78 % with both cameras.
  • Adding the action flow to the wrist camera raises it to 92 % / 77 %: close to the two-camera baseline on test, and better on train.
  • So the flow-conditioned policy's performance comes from the action flow conditioning, not from the image observations.
  • Interestingly, the baselines do surprisingly well on the episodes that failed in our data collection (42 %), while the flow-conditioned policies fail on them (7 %): they follow the flow, and the flow is of a failed replay.

Visualizations
